Simon Stevinplein, Bruges

Bruges, Belgium  ·  51.2116° N, 3.2280° E  ·  3:28 PM–3:38 PM  ·  2 photos

NoteLunch spot confirmed as Poules Moules restaurant on Simon Stevinplein via matching signage/menu in the photo. A second photo (three hours later) shows an ornate stone archway with a quartered heraldic shield; the exact building could not be confirmed, though the coordinates place it a block from Simon Stevinplein, near St Salvator's Cathedral.

Lunch landed at Poules Moules, tucked into a restored 17th-century stepped-gable house on Simon Stevinplein, a square that itself once sat atop Bruges' medieval butchers' hall before it was torn down in 1819, presumably once the smell became a civic concern. The square takes its current name from the bronze statue of Simon Stevin, the Bruges-born mathematician and engineer who pioneered decimal fractions, erected here in 1846 for reasons that surely made complete sense to the committee involved. True to its name, the restaurant's specialty is the classic Belgian pairing of mussels and fries -- the pot of moules and towering frites in the photo are about as textbook a Flemish lunch as it is possible to order.

The second photo in this cluster, taken later in the afternoon, shows a grand carved stone doorway crowned with a coat of arms -- evidently a merchant's house or institutional entrance in the same quarter near the cathedral, its specific identity lost to history, or at least to this particular photograph.

The Markt, Bruges

Bruges, Belgium  ·  51.2082° N, 3.2268° E  ·  4:10 PM  ·  1 photo

The Markt is Bruges at its most iconic: the Belfry rising on one side, the neo-Gothic Provinciaal Hof filling the other, the two of them apparently competing for attention and both winning. The Belfry itself was raised in phases from the 13th to the 15th century as a proud symbol of civic and guild independence, its 366-step climb passing the old treasury and historic carillon on the way to a panorama over the city's red rooftops -- part of the UNESCO-listed Belfries of Belgium and France, a club the Belfry very much deserves membership in.

Across the square, the white stone Provinciaal Hof looks far older than it actually is: it replaced the medieval Waterhalle, a covered dock where boats once unloaded goods directly into the market, and was built in neo-Gothic style between 1887 and 1920, now housing the interactive Historium museum, which is a great deal of history for one building to be responsible for. The group returned to the Markt after dark, too -- the second set of photos, taken near 22:50 under a deep blue sky, shows the square lit up and full of evening strollers beside the statue of Jan Breydel and Pieter de Coninck, leaders of the 1302 uprising against French rule, standing there looking pleased with themselves, and reasonably so.

Jan van Eyckplein, Bruges

Bruges, Belgium  ·  51.2046° N, 3.2246° E  ·  4:36 PM–11:27 PM  ·  23 photos

A short walk from the Markt, past another glimpse of the Belfry insisting on being seen down a side street, opens onto Jan van Eyckplein, the old inner harbour of medieval Bruges where merchant ships once unloaded directly into the city, back when that was considered convenient rather than a health hazard. The square is ringed by the Poortersloge, a 14th-century meeting hall for Bruges' merchant elite, and the late-Gothic Tolhuis, the old tollhouse where goods were assessed on arrival, presumably with a great deal of muttering.

At its centre stands the bronze statue of Jan van Eyck, unveiled in 1878 in the presence of King Leopold II, the painter shown in his signature turban, brush in one hand and a framed panel in the other -- a fitting monument to the Flemish master whose Ghent Altarpiece the group had admired only the day before, and had by now presumably stopped talking about.

Spiegelrei Canal, Bruges

Bruges, Belgium  ·  51.2090° N, 3.2241° E  ·  10:48 PM  ·  2 photos

NoteGeneral canal-front location confirmed via the Jan van Eyckplein/Spiegelrei area's distinctive stepped-gable row houses; individual café names in the photos were not researched.

Just off Jan van Eyckplein, the Spiegelrei canal runs west in a straight, tree-free line, deliberately so, since this was once open water where seagoing vessels moored before Bruges' link to the sea rather inconveniently silted up. The crow-stepped brick gables lining its banks, some carrying ornate Renaissance lintels, are among the most photographed rows in the city, and by now they know it.

It makes a fitting bookend to the van Eyck statue a few dozen metres away: this canal is the very reason Bruges became rich enough to commission painters like him in the first place, silt notwithstanding.
