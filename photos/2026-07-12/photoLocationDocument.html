Westerkerk / Westertoren, Prinsengracht

Amsterdam, Jordaan / Prinsengracht canal (viewed from a boat)  ·  52.3730° N, 4.8830° E  ·  6:05 PM  ·  1 photo

NoteThe blue-and-gold onion-domed crown atop the tower and the tower's coordinates (Westerkerk sits at roughly 52.3744N, 4.8836E, about 150m from this cluster's centroid, consistent with the shot being taken from the canal below) confirm this is the Westertoren, the tower of the Westerkerk. The Amsterdam 'XXX' shield visible partway up the tower face is the city's coat of arms.

At 85 metres, the Westertoren is the tallest church tower in Amsterdam, finished in 1638 to a design by Hendrick de Keyser (his son Pieter had to complete it after his father's death, presumably while muttering about deadlines). The crown on top isn't decorative flourish -- it's a replica of the imperial crown of Holy Roman Emperor Maximilian I, who granted Amsterdam the right to use his personal crown in its coat of arms after the city lent him money in 1489, which is a splendid reminder that gratitude, historically, has always been denominated in cash. That's also where the three St. Andrew's crosses (the 'XXX' visible on the tower) come from: the city's own arms, and not, as tourists persistently and hopefully guess, a reference to anything else.

The tower stands over the Jordaan, and its bells carry a long way -- Anne Frank, hiding two canals over, wrote in her diary about finding comfort in the Westertoren's carillon marking the hours, a fact that ought to keep anyone from making too many jokes about this particular tower. Rembrandt is buried somewhere inside the church itself, in an unmarked pauper's grave, the exact location lost, which is either a tragedy or the most Rembrandt thing imaginable. Seeing the tower from the water, as in this photo, is how most visitors first encounter it -- it announces the Jordaan long before a boat actually reaches the neighbourhood, rather like a very tall, very punctual usher.

NEMO Science Museum, Oosterdok

Amsterdam, Oosterdok / IJ waterfront  ·  52.3715° N, 4.9134° E  ·  6:57 PM  ·  1 photo

NoteThe large copper-green, ship-hulled building rising out of the water, with tall wooden sailing ships and houseboats moored in front, is unmistakably the NEMO Science Museum on the Oosterdok, matched by both the architecture and the cluster's coordinates near the Oosterdokseiland/Kattenburg waterfront.

This green hull rising out of the harbour is NEMO, the Netherlands' largest science museum, designed by Renzo Piano and opened in 1997. Piano had to build over the mouth of the IJ tunnel, and rather than fight the constraint, he leaned into it with theatrical enthusiasm: the building's curved shape echoes the tunnel below, and the whole structure insists on being read as a ship rising from the water rather than a building merely standing on land, which is a bold claim for a museum to make about itself. The rooftop, reachable by a long outdoor staircase that tests one's commitment to science, doubles as a public plaza with a shallow fountain and sweeping views back over the harbour.

The museum itself has a far older lineage than its 1997 building would suggest -- it began in 1923 as the Museum van den Arbeid (Museum of Labour), a hands-on institute for popularising industrial technique, and went through several identity crises (Nint, newMetropolis) before finally settling on NEMO. The wooden tall ships moored in front, visible in these photos, belong to the Oosterdok's working harbour tradition, a fitting foreground for a science museum still occupying what used to be a shipyard basin and, one suspects, rather proud of the irony.
